The decentralized administration of the internet is one of the basic enablers of the rapid growth of the Internet as a whole, thanks to the autonomous nature different network systems.
The different Internet Service Providers (ISPs) play a critical role in the functionality of the Internet, as they connect their end customers (Internet users) to the Internet backhaul.
The power dynamic of this system inherently gives control over shaping consumption patterns to the ISPs: consolidation of power at the hands of a few ISPs gives them the unique ability to control, throttle, or monitor the flow of information, in addition to enabling oppressive practices such as censorship and surveillance of end users.
Most recently, communication distruptions due to airstrikes on main fiber optic lines and radio sites, and the depletion of fuel for generators, have severely limited the information flow to the population of Gaza since the start of the war in October 2023, halting humanitarian coordination, and leaving the affected population entirely cut off from life-saving information and assistance (\cite{gaza-brief},~\cite{gaza-report}).
Not only physical disruptions, but also governmental control of the telecommunication infrastructure often leads to the repression of communities during times of political unrest (\cite{digital-repression-palestine},~\cite{internet-shutdown-human-rights}), severely limiting the possibility for social actors to dissent and organize themselves.
Together, the aforementioned practices have lead to a growing interest in peer-to-peer (p2p) messaging platforms in recent years.
Most notably, the rise of p2p messaging platforms like Firechat (cite), Briar (cite), and Amigo (cite), have proven that there is a growing shift in user interest patterns away from traditional Centralized Social Networks towards diverse p2p social networks.
Physical p2p networks are limited by the communication radius supported by the underlying hardware and protocols, and are characterised by multihop communication.
There are a plethora of protocols to use for p2p communication, and we chose to focus on stable ones that are supported by most devices used by the populace in disaster scenarios: virtually everyone has a smartphone nowadays that supports Bluetooth (cite smartphone ubiquoity?), and the situation in refugee camps and disaster zones is not different.
We realised that by focusing on low-power communication, we can extend device battery life to allow for critical communication in extreme situations: we thus decided to use Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) for our p2p communication protocol, which allows users to exchange messages over short ranges.
To achieve a higher level of information decentralization, resilience to topology changes, we decided to focus on unstructured p2p networks as they are more suitable for dynamic alternative communication methods often necessary to guarantee free speech in disaster zones, protests, and for citizens in oppressive regimes.
The research community has proposed many metrics to measure the centralization, sparsity, and resilience of ISPs, web hosting providers, certificate authorities, and signals (\cite{dependence},~\cite{sparsity}), algorithms for selecting the most appropriate communication mode in Bluetooth 5~\cite{adaptive-phy}, and different signal propagation path loss models~\cite{pathloss}.
Most recently, systems and protocols were introduced to enable secure p2p communication between smartphones (\cite{amigo},~\cite{bitchat}), but none of these works evaluate the metrics that prove those systems are actually resilient and appropriate to use in extreme real-world situations.
In this research paper, we conduct a comprehensive representational experiment to collect empirical data on the stochastics of the BLE signal propagation, and use the collected real-world empirical evidence to parameterize simulations of unstructured p2p networks, and then measure their centralization~\cite{dependence}, sparsity~\cite{sparsity}, and theoretical capacity.
