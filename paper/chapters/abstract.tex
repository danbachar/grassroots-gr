Recent crises, natural disasters, and large-scale protests have shown that people heavily rely on online social networks and instant messaging platforms to communicate, while organizations use those platforms to coordinate aid efforts. During crises, communication networks can be strained, partially or wholly destroyed, censored, or otherwise monitored, leaving reliant communities in need of alternatives.
The existing Internet infrastructure is distributed, but its management and ownership are consolidated. A free distributed alternative to consolidated social networks are peer-to-peer networks, which distribute the power and content control over many, equal peers.
Previous scholarship has evaluated the metrics and performance of structured overlays of p2p networks. Building on that research, this paper uniquely focuses on evaluating sparsity and resilience metrics of unstructured p2p networks, which are crucial for opportunistic networking in crises.
By providing empirical and simulated boundaries on the performance, resilience, and reliability metrics, this research supports the design of more robust, scalable self-healing social networks that are resilient to duress. Those networks have been proven to persevere in light of emergencies and are resistant to censorship, providing a way to communicate freely and reliably using opportunistic networking.
In this paper, we investigate resilience and reliability metrics for both simulated as well as real-life unstructured peer-to-peer social networks, in an attempt to characterise the performance, deliverability, throughput, as well as reliable range for applications.
